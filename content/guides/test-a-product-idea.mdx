Most product ideas don't fail because they were bad ideas. They fail because nobody tested them before a lot of time and money went in. By the time the market gives its verdict, the founder has already built a case for the idea in their own head — and that case is very hard to argue against, even with your own eyes.

Testing an idea before you build it isn't about being clever or having access to some secret research technique. It's about doing three unglamorous things in order, and refusing to skip ahead.

## Write the idea down properly

If you can't describe your idea in four sentences — who it's for, what problem it solves, how they solve that problem today, and why now is the right time — you don't have an idea yet. You have a feeling.

This matters more than it sounds like it should. Vague ideas are comfortable because they're never wrong. "A better way for small teams to collaborate" can absorb any evidence you throw at it. "A shared task list specifically for five-person agencies juggling client deadlines across email and Slack" can be tested and can be wrong — which is exactly what you want. An idea that can be wrong is an idea you can learn something from.

Use a structured one-pager for this rather than a blank document. The constraint of a fixed set of questions — customer, problem, current alternative, riskiest assumption — stops you from writing marketing copy about your idea instead of a testable description of it.

## Work out how much evidence you actually need

Not every idea deserves the same amount of scrutiny. A tool that copies a proven model into a new geography or niche needs far less validation than something that asks people to behave in a genuinely new way. Conflating the two is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes early founders make — either over-validating something obvious, or charging ahead on something that needed far more evidence than a two-week sprint could gather.

We use a simple three-way split for this at Incy Templates: is the idea **Proven** (a working model, applied somewhere new), **Better** (a real improvement on an existing, working approach), or **New** (asking people to do something they don't currently do at all)? Each category comes with a different bar for evidence before you commit real time. A Proven idea might only need a handful of conversations to confirm local fit. A New idea needs you to find people already trying to solve the problem badly, by hand, today — because if nobody is doing that, the problem may not be painful enough yet.

Get this classification wrong in either direction and you'll waste months: either endlessly validating something that was already obviously going to work, or building confidently on an assumption nobody has actually confirmed.

## Go find the evidence, not the encouragement

This is the step most founders quietly skip, because it's uncomfortable. Showing people a description of your idea and asking "would you use this?" produces encouragement, not evidence — people are polite, and hypothetical enthusiasm costs them nothing.

Real evidence looks different. It's a person describing, unprompted, the workaround they currently use and how much it annoys them. It's someone asking when they can pay you, without being asked if they'd pay. It's a waiting list that fills up from an honest description of the problem, not from a slick landing page. If you can't find any of that within a week or two of genuinely looking, that's real information too — and it's a lot cheaper to receive now than after three months of building.

Keep a simple running log that separates what you're assuming from what you've actually confirmed. It sounds basic, but the discipline of writing "assumption" next to something you believe but haven't checked is one of the few reliable defences against your own optimism.

## What to do with the answer

Testing an idea should end in a decision, not just a folder of notes. Once you've classified the idea and gathered what evidence you reasonably can, force yourself to write down one of three things: proceed, revise the idea based on what you learned, or pause it and work on something else.

"Pause" is not failure. An idea correctly identified as not worth pursuing, after a focused week of real testing, is a good outcome — you've saved yourself the months you would otherwise have spent finding that out the hard way. The founders who waste the most time aren't the ones who test ideas and reject a few. They're the ones who never really test anything at all.
