Most people's honest reaction to a new meeting invite is closer to dread than enthusiasm — and often for good reason. Around half of what happens in a typical meeting isn't relevant to most attendees, or could have been handled without a meeting at all. The fix isn't better meeting etiquette. It's fewer, better-chosen meetings, run well.

## Fewer meetings, topics and participants

Before accepting or proposing a meeting, ask what it's actually for, what it costs (not just the calendar time — the preparation, the context-switching, the dead time before and after), and whether a better alternative exists. A useful distinction: **star interactions** — one person broadcasting to everyone else, or a genuinely one-on-one conversation — are best handled outside a group meeting entirely. **Spaghetti interactions** — real back-and-forth between several people — are what meetings are actually for. If your meeting is mostly star-shaped, it probably shouldn't be a meeting.

## Nine rules for the meetings you do have

Ray Dalio's rules, condensed: clarify who owns the meeting and its goal; match your communication style to the objective — a debate needs different handling than an update; balance assertiveness with genuinely hearing less experienced voices; prevent topic drift by tracking progress against the stated purpose; anchor disagreements in facts — "is it true?" beats "I feel"; assign clear owners and deadlines, since shared responsibility usually means no responsibility; give people two uninterrupted minutes to make their point; slow down fast talkers who use speed to dodge scrutiny; and always end with a decision, a summary, or a confirmed next step — never just "more discussion needed."

## Using the process

The **Meeting Agenda Template** turns a meeting's purpose into a documented agenda — outcomes, process, participants and time allocation — before it happens, not during it. The **Meeting Usefulness Diagnostic** Tool asks a few quick questions about a specific meeting and tells you whether to keep it, trim the invite list, replace it with an async update, or cancel it outright.

This isn't a one-off audit. Revisit it whenever a recurring meeting's purpose or attendee list starts to drift — most bad meeting habits creep in gradually, not all at once.
