Better Decision Maker gets you a decision to proceed. The next failure mode is scope creep: every feature seems reasonable on its own, and the pile of reasonable features quietly becomes a six-month build instead of a two-week one. Scoping an MVP isn't about building less for its own sake — it's about building only what actually earns its place before you've learned anything.

## Start from your riskiest open question

Every product idea rests on at least one assumption you're genuinely unsure about — will people actually use this, will they pay for it, will they come back a second time. That's your riskiest open question, and it should drive scope more than anything else. A feature that directly answers it belongs in the MVP even if it's inconvenient to build. A feature that doesn't touch it at all is a candidate for cutting, no matter how reasonable it sounds in a planning meeting.

## Separate necessity from nice-to-have

For each candidate feature, ask a blunt question: does the product actually fail to deliver its core value without this, or would people just not notice? Founders routinely mistake "this would be nice" for "this is necessary" — usually because a nice feature is easy to picture working well, while the boring necessary ones don't inspire the same enthusiasm. Necessity and enthusiasm are not the same thing.

## Weigh effort honestly

A feature that's both necessary and cheap to build is an easy call. The genuinely hard decisions are the necessary-but-expensive ones and the nice-but-cheap ones — and the second category is where most scope creep actually comes from, because "it's basically free" quietly bypasses the necessity question entirely. Weigh effort against what the feature is actually for, not against how easy it feels to say yes to.

## Ask what you could fake instead

Before committing engineering time to anything, ask whether you could deliver it by hand first — a concierge process, a manual spreadsheet, a one-off email instead of an automated one. Faking a feature is often the single fastest way to learn whether it's worth building at all, and it costs almost nothing to reverse if you're wrong. Reserve real engineering effort for the things you've already confirmed matter, or the things too central to fake even temporarily.

## Using the process

The **MVP Scope in One Page** template forces the whole exercise onto a single page for your entire first release: what's in, what's explicitly out, and the one question it needs to answer. The **Scope Decider** Tool applies the same four questions — necessity, relevance to your riskiest question, build effort, and fakeability — to one candidate feature at a time, and gives you a Keep, Defer or Remove verdict plus a concrete next step. Run it once per feature you're unsure about; the one-page template is where the resulting list actually lives.

Once your scope is locked, the next step is usually working out what to call the thing you're about to build — that's what Product Naming System is for.
