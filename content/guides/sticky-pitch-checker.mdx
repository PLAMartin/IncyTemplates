A pitch can be accurate, well-reasoned, and still forgotten five minutes after you deliver it. Most pitches don't fail because the idea behind them is weak — they fail because nothing about *how* they're presented makes them memorable, or worth repeating to someone else. Two things have to be true for a pitch to actually work: it has to stick in someone's head, and it has to be worth passing on. Ten factors, from two well-known frameworks, cover both.

## What makes it stick (SUCCESs)

> "The generation of ideas that are sticky ... may seem like magic, but ... it is a skill that can be studied and taught." — Chip & Dan Heath, *Made to Stick*

**Simple.** Boil the pitch down to one core, profound idea. If you try to make several points at once, none of them will be remembered — the audience doesn't know where to focus. JFK's "put a man on the moon and return him safely by the end of the decade" is simple without being simplistic.

**Unexpected.** Break the pattern people expect. Surprise gets attention; opening a genuine gap in someone's knowledge — a hook — is what keeps it, the same way a good mystery story works.

**Concrete.** Speak in terms of human actions and the five senses, not generic mission-statement language. Concrete imagery — an apple falling on Newton's head — means the idea means the same thing to everyone who hears it.

**Credible.** Back the idea with something specific: a demonstration, an internally consistent detail, a real test case. Authority alone (trust me, I'm an expert) is the weakest version of credibility, not the strongest.

**Emotional.** Get people to feel something, not just understand it. Appeal to identity, or to something they already care about, and describe benefits rather than features — car adverts sell a feeling, not a spec sheet.

**Story.** Wrap the idea in a story people can mentally rehearse. Firefighters swap stories after emergencies for exactly this reason: hearing one helps you respond better when your own version of it happens.

## What makes it spread (STEPPS)

> "People don't think in terms of information. They think in terms of narratives." — Jonah Berger, *Contagious*

Two of Berger's six STEPPS factors — Emotion and Stories — are the same ideas SUCCESs already covers above, so they're not repeated here. The other four are genuinely additional:

**Social Currency.** Give people a way to look clever, cool or in-the-know for sharing it. Something remarkable, novel or a little exclusive is worth being the one who talked about it first.

**Triggers.** Tie the idea to something already in your audience's everyday environment, so it comes to mind at the moment it's actually useful — not just the one time they first heard it.

**Public.** Make the idea's use visible to others. The more observable something is — a logo facing outward, a badge someone can see — the more it triggers someone else to try it too.

**Practical Value.** Package the value so it's easy to pass on. A specific, quantified detail — a percentage, a stat — travels further than a vague claim that something is "better."

## Using the process

The **Sticky Pitch Worksheet** is a one-page worksheet for checking all ten factors against a specific pitch before you deliver it. The **Pitch Stickiness Check** Tool asks ten yes/no questions about that same pitch and tells you which factor, if any, still needs work.

This isn't a one-off exercise. Run it on every real pitch — to a customer, an investor, or your own team — not just the first one.
