The Product Idea Assessor and Customer Discovery Kit get you evidence. At some point you have to actually decide something — and the evidence is rarely complete enough to make the decision obvious. Waiting for certainty is itself a decision, usually the wrong one. Here's a repeatable process for deciding well without it.

## Classify the decision by reversibility first

Before you weigh anything, ask one question: if this turns out to be wrong, how easily can I undo it? A **two-way door** decision — trying a new pricing page, testing a new channel — costs little to reverse, so the right move is usually to decide fast and let reality correct you. A **one-way door** decision — signing a long lease, hiring a co-founder, taking on debt — deserves real analysis before you walk through it, because backing out is expensive or impossible.

Most founders instinctively over-analyse two-way doors and under-analyse one-way doors, because the two-way doors are the decisions that come up most often and feel more urgent in the moment. Classifying first fixes the ordering: it tells you how much of the rest of this process a given decision actually deserves.

## Invert the question

Instead of asking "what would make this succeed," ask "what would guarantee this fails." It's usually a much shorter, more concrete list — running out of money before you learn anything, building something nobody asked for, picking a co-founder whose values don't match yours. Once you can see the failure modes clearly, the decision often simplifies to "avoid these specific things," which is a far easier question to answer than "find the best possible option."

Inversion is most useful exactly when a decision feels genuinely hard to compare on its merits — it changes the question from an abstract comparison into a concrete checklist of what to avoid.

## Write down your simple rules

Some decisions don't need fresh analysis every time — they need a rule, decided once, applied consistently. "We only add a feature if three or more paying customers have separately asked for it." "We don't take on custom work that isn't repeatable." A good simple rule is short enough to remember, specific enough to actually constrain a choice, and written down before the pressure of a live decision can bend it.

Simple rules exist to save your judgement for the decisions that genuinely need it, by removing the ones that don't.

## Estimate expected value, then let reversibility break a tie

Once you have real options to compare, expected value gives you a way to compare them on the same terms: how likely is each one to work, how big is the payoff if it does, and how much would it cost to attempt. A high-likelihood, high-impact, low-effort option beats a low-likelihood, small-impact, high-effort one — obviously — but expected value is most useful precisely when the comparison *isn't* obvious.

When two options come out close, don't agonise further — go back to reversibility. If one of the close options is a two-way door, take it; you'll learn more from trying it than from further analysis, and it costs little to be wrong. If both are one-way doors and the numbers are genuinely close, that's a signal to go gather more evidence before committing to either, not a signal to guess.

## Using the process

The **Decision Worksheet** walks through all four steps on paper for a single decision — reversibility, inversion, your relevant simple rules, and an expected-value estimate — and ends with a documented decision and reasoning you can revisit later. The **Expected Value Comparator** Tool does the last step interactively: score two options against each other and get a recommendation, a confidence read on how clear the gap is, and one next step drawn straight from the reversibility framing above.

Once you've decided, the next step is usually working out the smallest way to test that decision before committing fully — that's what MVP Scoper is for.
