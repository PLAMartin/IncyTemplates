Sarah Willingham walked into a meeting two minutes late. A lawyer looked up and said, "Thank goodness. Mine's a flat white with one sugar." She smiled, made him the coffee, brought it back, and asked if anyone else wanted one — then sat down and waited while he realised what he'd done. She used the moment to secure the deal. That's a story. "I once had an awkward moment in a meeting that worked out well" is a description. The difference is structure, not talent.

## The five-part spine

**Place**: start with location — one clear noun the reader can stand in, like "airport" or "boardroom." Don't drown them in decor; naming the place does the work. **Action**: use verbs. What are you doing right now, in the scene? The story should already be happening, not about to start. **Thought**: let the reader hear your head, not your résumé — the raw, slightly messy thought you actually had, not a tidy summary of the feeling. **Emotion, shown**: don't name it, show the body doing something — a shaking hand, a too-quick laugh. Let the reader supply the label. **Dialogue**: let other people in the scene talk. One specific line does more work than a sentence describing how someone felt.

Put those five together — Place, Action, Thought, Emotion, Dialogue — and you have a story spine. Add detail where it helps, cut where it doesn't.
