The Product Idea Assessor tells you how much evidence an idea needs. This is about actually going and getting it. Customer discovery has a specific failure mode: you ask questions shaped by what you already believe, the other person picks up on that shape and answers agreeably, and you walk away with your existing view intact, now labelled "validated." The interview felt productive. It wasn't.

The Customer Discovery Kit is the plan-run-log-score cycle that avoids that trap.

## Plan who to talk to and what you'll ask

Start with a target list: who, specifically, and why them. Then build a script that asks about the past and present, not the future. "Tell me about the last time you dealt with this problem" asks for a memory, which is hard to fake and comes with detail — what actually happened, what they tried, how long it took, whether it worked. "Would you use this" asks for a prediction, and people are bad at predicting their own behaviour, and polite besides. The **Customer Interview Planner** template builds this script for you, including the target-list worksheet.

## Run the interview without contaminating it

Get as far as you can into understanding someone's actual situation before you say anything about what you're building. In many good interviews you never mention it at all — the moment you describe your idea, the interviewee knows what answer would please you, consciously or not. When a first answer is surface-level ("we use a spreadsheet for that"), ask "why" one or two levels deeper than feels natural: why a spreadsheet and not the tool that's supposedly built for this? What did they try before it?

## Log evidence separately from what you're assuming

Directly after each interview, write one sentence: what did I hear that I didn't expect? Keep that running list somewhere separate from your task list — a place whose entire job is separating what you're assuming from what you've actually confirmed. The **Assumption and Evidence Tracker** template is exactly that: start it the day you begin discovery and keep it updated as evidence comes in.

## Know when you've heard enough

You don't need a statistically significant sample to make a good early decision — you need to stop hearing new things. When three or four interviews in a row confirm the same pattern with no new surprises, you probably have enough to act on. When every interview still surfaces something unexpected, keep going.

For the full technique — including how to structure the opening minutes and how to avoid the most common leading-question traps — see [How to Run a Customer Interview That Actually Changes Your Mind](/guides/run-a-customer-interview-that-changes-your-mind).

## Scoring what you've gathered

Once you've run a first round, the question shifts from "what did I hear" to "can I actually trust it." The **Evidence Analyser** Tool scores exactly that: how many people you spoke to, how open your questions really were, whether what you heard was real past behaviour or just opinion, whether anyone showed a costly commitment, and whether a pattern is actually forming across interviews.

The one dimension that overrides all the others is question style. If your questions were mostly leading, the Tool caps the score and flags a bias-risk warning regardless of how strong the other answers look — a consistent pattern collected through leading questions isn't evidence of the problem, it's evidence that people are polite. Fix that first, before trusting anything else you've heard.

Once the Tool says your evidence is strong enough to trust, the next step is turning it into a decision — that's what the Better Decision Maker family is for.
