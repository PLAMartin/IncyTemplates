Most founder journeys assume you already have an idea. This one doesn't. Waiting for inspiration to arrive is slow and unreliable — a repeatable process beats a blank page every time, and it's the same process whether you're looking for your first idea or your fiftieth.

## Feed the input

Ideas rarely appear from nothing; they come from combining things you already know in a new way. A simple five-step process makes that combination more likely to happen: **gather** material loosely related to a problem or topic that interests you; **digest** it — turn it over, look for patterns and connections rather than trying to force an answer; **step away** and do something unrelated that clears your head, since forcing it rarely works; let ideas **bubble up** on their own, and write them down the moment they do, before they're gone; then **test** the idea by putting it in front of other people and being open to how it needs to change. None of these steps is optional — skipping "step away" in particular is the most common reason people feel stuck.

## Three ways to find a real idea

If you want a starting point rather than waiting for something to bubble up, there are three reliable places to look. **Scratch your own itch** — build something that solves a problem you genuinely have yourself. You're the ideal test user: you already understand the problem, and if it bothers you, it likely bothers others too. **Address a niche** — go deep on a specific group of people you already understand from the inside, whether through a hobby, a job or a community, and solve something for them specifically rather than trying to please everyone a little. **Improve an existing product** — you don't need a totally new idea; take something people already use and already pay for, and work out what it still gets wrong. Ready-made demand is a head start, not a shortcut around doing the work.

## Build the daily habit

The single most useful habit for idea generation is writing ideas down every day, without judging them as you go. Start small — even one a day is enough to begin — and resist the urge to filter as you write; judgement comes later, once you have enough ideas to compare. The ideas themselves don't have to be about products: topics to write about, ways to fix a process, questions worth asking. The habit is what matters. It rewires how much you notice, and the product ideas follow.

## Using the process

The **Idea Capture Log** template gives you a place to run this daily practice properly — one entry per idea, tagged by which of the three sources it came from, reviewed weekly rather than judged on the spot. The **Idea Direction Generator** Tool takes whatever you already have — a frustration, a niche you know well, a product you use and think could be better — and turns it into a concrete idea direction and a first test step, using your own words rather than a generic prompt.

Neither of these tells you whether an idea is *good*. Once you have a direction that feels real, take it to **Product Idea Assessor** — that's where you find out how much evidence it actually needs before you commit real time to it.
