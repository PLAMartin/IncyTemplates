Better Decision Maker walks you through one process — reversibility, inversion, your own rules, expected value — for a single consequential decision. This is different: most days you're not facing one big call, you're facing a lot of smaller, differently-shaped ones, and the same technique doesn't fit all of them. This is a reference for picking the right tool for whatever's actually in front of you, not a replacement for the deeper process when a decision genuinely warrants it.

## Six Thinking Hats

Edward de Bono's Six Thinking Hats gives you six deliberate perspectives to cycle through: Red (feelings and intuition), Black (caution and risk), White (facts, no opinion), Yellow (benefits and optimism), Green (new ideas), Blue (stepping back to plan and organise). Switching hats on purpose stops you — or a group — getting stuck arguing from one angle. It's most useful when other people are involved, or when a decision genuinely has several valid ways to look at it and you keep circling the same one.

## First Principles Thinking

Most decisions get made by analogy — "this worked before, so it should work again." First principles thinking, the approach Elon Musk describes using, means boiling a problem down to the facts you're actually certain of and reasoning back up from there, ignoring how it's normally done. It takes more mental effort than reasoning by analogy, but it's the only option when there's no existing precedent close enough to copy.

## Razors

A razor is a rule of thumb that trades a little accuracy for a lot of speed — useful precisely because most decisions don't deserve deep analysis. A few worth knowing: the Luck Razor (between similar options, pick the one that opens more future doors), the Hourly Rate Razor (set a personal hourly rate; anything cheaper to outsource than that rate, outsource), the Stress v Benefit Razor (only take on something stressful if the potential benefit clearly justifies it), and Hofstadter's Law (things take longer than expected, even accounting for Hofstadter's Law). Reach for a razor when the decision is small, low-stakes, and likely to come up again.

## The Boundary Rule

Some decisions aren't a single choice — they're a sequence: candidates for a role, houses to view, vendors to compare. The classic answer is a boundary rule: review roughly the first third of the options you expect to see without choosing any of them, purely to calibrate what "good" looks like, then pick the very next one that beats everything you've seen so far. It won't guarantee the single best option, but it reliably avoids the two failure modes — stopping too early or searching forever.
