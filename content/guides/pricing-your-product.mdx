Product/Market Fit Tracker tells you people want what you've built. It doesn't tell you how to charge for it — and most founders skip straight past that question, defaulting to whatever pricing model looks normal in their category (subscription, because that's what SaaS does) without checking whether it actually fits how the product delivers value. Get the model wrong and no number inside it will ever feel right; the model matters more than the price.

## Find your value metric

Start with what actually scales value for a customer. Some products have an obvious countable unit — seats, API calls, projects, storage — where more usage clearly means more value received. Others don't: a habit-tracking app or a one-off design tool delivers roughly the same value to everyone, regardless of how often they open it. If you can't name the unit, usage-based and tiered pricing don't have anything real to hang off, and you're better off with something simpler.

## Match the model to how customers actually use it

Ask whether the product delivers value once or repeatedly. A subscription is a promise to keep delivering value on an ongoing basis — charge one when the product only solves a single job, and you're asking customers to pay indefinitely for something they've already finished using. That mismatch shows up fast, as cancellations and refund requests, not as revenue. A one-off job deserves a one-off price; ongoing value deserves a recurring one.

## Price for who's actually buying

The buyer shapes what's viable. An individual buying for themselves wants a price they can decide on alone, quickly. A small business can absorb a bit more complexity if it maps to something they recognise. An enterprise buyer often expects a formal, tiered structure with room to negotiate — a single flat price can look under-specified to a procurement process that's used to choosing between options.

## Use price discrimination and relative pricing deliberately

Price discrimination — charging different customers different amounts for essentially the same thing — isn't a trick, it's how you avoid leaving money on the table with customers who'd happily pay more, while still winning the ones who wouldn't. Tiers are the most common way to do this without haggling: offer a deliberately basic tier, a deliberately expensive one, and the one in the middle you actually want most people to choose. Customers judge each tier relative to the others, not in isolation, so the tiers you build shape which one looks like the obvious choice. This only works when your price isn't directly, easily compared to a competitor's — if it is, added complexity mostly just makes you harder to compare, and a simple, visibly competitive price wins instead.
