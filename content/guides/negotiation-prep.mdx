Most people walk into a negotiation having thought hard about what they want, and barely at all about how they're going to get it. They know their number. They don't know what they'll do if the other side won't move, what to say first, or how to offer flexibility without looking like they'll cave on anything. The difference between negotiating from strength and negotiating from hope usually comes down to three specific things, all preparable well before you sit down.

## Fallback options (BATNA)

> "The reason you negotiate is to produce something better than the results you can obtain without negotiating." — Roger Fisher & William Ury

Your BATNA — Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement — is what you'll do if this negotiation doesn't produce a deal. If you're happy with the car you already have, you don't need to buy another one. A strong BATNA boosts your confidence and gives you real leverage: research shows negotiators with good alternatives aim higher and secure better outcomes. Real power comes from having solid alternatives *before* you enter the discussion, not from anything you say during it.

No deal is better than a bad one. Knowing exactly what "no deal" looks like for you — concretely, not just as a vague sense of discomfort — is what lets you walk away from a bad offer instead of accepting it out of momentum.

## Anchoring

> "The starting point you give someone is not just a suggestion. It becomes a psychological benchmark." — Richard Thaler

The first number mentioned in a negotiation sets the tone. Most people adjust only slightly from that initial figure — people think in increments, so a £100K anchor shifts the negotiation in £20K swings, while a £10K anchor might limit it to £2K moves. That's why you anchor first, and anchor high if you're selling (low if you're buying): set the pace with confidence rather than waiting to react to theirs.

## Multiple Equivalent Simultaneous Offers (MESOs)

> "Options reduce resistance. Give them three ways to say yes and they'll forget how to say no." — Chris Voss

This tactic means presenting two or three offers at once, each with different trade-offs but all acceptable to you. Renting out a property, you might offer: standard rent for 12 months, a lower rent for 24 months, or a higher rent with an option to buy after 12. MESOs let you uncover what the other party values most without asking them directly — you appear collaborative and flexible while subtly steering the negotiation, and if they try to cherry-pick the best parts of each offer, you can guide the discussion toward a new option that still works for you.

## Using the process

The **Negotiation Prep Sheet** is a one-page worksheet for filling in all three tactics against a specific upcoming negotiation before you sit down. The **Negotiation Readiness Check** Tool asks up to three questions about that same negotiation and tells you which tactic, if any, still needs work.

This isn't a one-off exercise. Every negotiation is different — run the check again for the next one, whether it's a supplier contract, a salary, or a partnership term sheet.
