"The most serious mistakes are not being made as a result of wrong answers," Peter Drucker once said. "The true dangerous thing is asking the wrong question." A vague prompt gets a vague answer back — not because the chatbot can't help, but because it was never told enough to help well.

## The CARE framework

Treat a prompt like an instruction to a clever but literal assistant. **Context** — who or what should it act as, and what's the situation? **Action** — what should it actually do? **Result** — what format or output do you want back? **Example** — a model to guide the style, if you have one. Put together: "You're a nutritionist (Context). Create a 7-day vegetarian meal plan (Action) with calorie counts and recipes, in table format (Result). Example: Day 1 – [sample]." Specific beats vague every time — "Summarise key events of World War I in 200 words for a 10 year old" gets a far better answer than "Tell me about history."

A few things sharpen it further: don't settle for the first result — if it's too vague, add specificity; if it's off-topic, rephrase; if it's too much, split it into smaller prompts. Give the chatbot feedback directly, the way you'd coach a colleague: "too formal — make it friendlier." And for harder tasks, reach for a role ("act as a salary negotiation coach"), a worked example to set the style, or breaking one big ask into a chain of smaller ones.

## Letting the chatbot ask the questions

Most people use a chatbot like an enhanced search engine — ask a question, get an answer. There's a more powerful move: flip it. Explain the problem and the outcome you're after, then end with a simple instruction: "Now ask me one question at a time, waiting for my answer in between, to help me think this through." The chatbot stops handing you answers and starts drawing out your own thinking — each question forces clarity, each answer exposes an assumption you hadn't fully articulated. It becomes less like a vending machine for answers and more like a coach.

## Using the process

The **Prompt Template** is a printable CARE worksheet, plus three ready-made patterns you can reuse directly: "Explain [topic] simply, with 2 real-world examples," "Generate [n] ideas for [task], each tailored to [audience]," and "Compare [option A] vs [B] by [criteria], in table form." The **Prompt Builder** Tool asks four quick questions — Context, Action, Result and an optional Example — plus whether to add the question-flip instruction, and hands you back a ready-to-paste prompt.

Prompting is a skill, not a one-off task. Keep a few prompts that worked well, and reuse them as your own personal templates.
