Edward de Bono coined the term Lateral Thinking to describe a different mode of thought from ordinary, step-by-step logic — one that deliberately departs from the obvious path, embracing paradox and contradiction as a route to genuinely new ideas rather than a distraction from them. You don't need a creative personality to use it. You need a handful of specific techniques, applied on purpose, to a problem you're currently stuck on.

## Five techniques

**Perceptual change**: look at the problem from a different position entirely. Uber realised the wait for a taxi mattered less than the anxiety of not knowing when it would arrive — so they let you watch the car approach on a map, and the wait itself felt shorter. **Random input**: force a connection between your problem and something completely unrelated. Constraints and randomness break the patterns your brain defaults to. **Provocation**: deliberately state the opposite of what you believe and ask what if it were true. Airbnb's founders challenged the assumption that strangers wouldn't want to stay in each other's homes — and built a company on being wrong about that. **Specificity**: swap generic description for one small, concrete, sensory detail — the thing that actually makes an idea vivid instead of forgettable. **Scale**: push the problem to its extremes. What does the smallest possible version look like? The biggest? What changes if it happened instantly, or took ten years?

## Generate more than you need

You can't have good ideas without being willing to have a lot of bad ones first. A pottery class split into two groups — one told to make thirty pots across the month, the other told to spend the whole month perfecting one — and every one of the best pots came from the group that made thirty. Volume beats premature perfectionism. Don't judge a prompt's output while you're generating it; judge afterwards, once you've got several to compare.
