In the 1950s, Dieter Rams joined Braun, then a modest post-war electronics firm. He proposed a radically minimal radio, stripped of ornament and focused on function. His boss protested: "It looks unfinished." Dieter replied: "It looks honest." That design became a bestseller and marked the start of a design revolution — over the next thirty years Rams transformed Braun's radios, shavers and speakers into sleek, intuitive, timeless tools, and later became a direct influence on Steve Jobs and Apple's own minimalist philosophy. His core idea: good design is as little design as possible.

## The ten principles

Rams laid down ten qualities good design embodies. **Innovative** — imaginative design develops in tandem with improving technology, never as an end in itself. **Useful** — a product is bought to be used; good design emphasises usefulness over anything that detracts from it. **Aesthetic** — aesthetic quality is integral to usefulness, but only well-executed objects can be beautiful. **Understandable** — it clarifies the product's structure; at best, it's self-explanatory. **Unobtrusive** — like a tool, its design should be neutral and restrained, leaving room for the user's own self-expression. **Honest** — it doesn't make a product seem more innovative, powerful or valuable than it really is. **Long-lasting** — it avoids being fashionable, so it never appears antiquated. **Thorough** — nothing is arbitrary or left to chance; care in the process shows respect for the user. **Environmentally friendly** — it conserves resources and minimises pollution across the product's life. **As little design as possible** — less, but better, concentrated on the essential and unburdened by non-essentials.

## Applying it

Rams himself framed the underlying test simply: "Indifference towards people and the reality in which they live is the one and only cardinal sin in design." Applied to a real product — a web tool that surfaces a new startup idea every day — the ten principles might look like this: a fresh approach to trend-spotting rather than a generic feed (innovative); every element serving the core task of finding a viable idea quickly (useful); clean typography and a calm, consistent layout (aesthetic); an interface people grasp immediately (understandable); the daily idea taking centre stage, not the chrome around it (unobtrusive); no over-promising about what the tool can actually find (honest); function over passing UI trends, so it won't look dated in a year (long-lasting); concise tags and readable fonts, cared for in the details (thorough); a lightweight structure that keeps server load low (environmentally friendly); and an interface stripped down to its essence (as little design as possible).

## Using the process

The **Design Checklist** is a printable, one-page version of all ten principles, for reviewing a specific product or screen before you ship a meaningful change. The **Design Self-Assessment** Tool asks ten quick yes/no questions, one per principle, and gives you a tip for whichever one is still missing first.

Revisit this every time you ship something meaningful, not once. Good design isn't a state you reach — it's a standard you keep checking your work against.
