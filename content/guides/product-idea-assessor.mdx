Every idea needs evidence before you commit real time or money to it — but not the same amount. The Copy–Improve–Differentiate method is how we classify an idea before deciding how hard to push on validating it, and it runs through the Product Idea Assessor's Template and Tool.

## Copy

A Copy idea applies a model that already demonstrably works — somewhere, for someone — to a new context: a new geography, a new niche, a new customer segment. The core mechanism isn't in question; what's untested is whether it transfers to your specific situation. A subscription box model that works well in the US, applied to a new category in the UK, is a Copy. A booking system for a type of service that already books appointments online everywhere except your target industry is a Copy.

Copy ideas need the least validation, but "least" doesn't mean none. You still need to confirm the specific transfer holds: that your target customers behave similarly to the customers the model already works for, and that nothing about your context breaks the mechanism. A handful of conversations confirming local fit is often enough.

## Improve

An Improve idea takes an existing, working approach and improves on a specific, articulable weakness — it's faster, cheaper, simpler, or removes a known point of friction, but the underlying customer behaviour is already established. People already do the thing your product helps them do; you're proposing a better way to do it, not a new behaviour altogether.

Improve ideas need evidence on two fronts: that the weakness you've identified in the existing approach is real and painful (not just annoying to you personally), and that your specific improvement actually resolves it for people who aren't you. It's common to be right about the weakness and wrong about whether your fix is the one people actually want — so test both separately if you can.

## Differentiate

A Differentiate idea asks people to adopt a genuinely new behaviour — not a better version of something they already do, but something they don't currently do at all, even in a clumsy or manual form. This is the highest-risk category, and it's also the one founders most often misclassify as Improve because the discomfort of admitting "nobody is currently doing this, even badly" is real.

The single most useful test for a Differentiate idea is to go looking for the manual, ugly, ad hoc version of the behaviour you're proposing. If people are already cobbling together spreadsheets, WhatsApp groups or paper processes to approximate what your product would do, that's a strong signal the underlying need is real, even though no polished solution exists yet. If you can't find any trace of people trying to solve this problem for themselves, that's important information too — it doesn't mean the idea is wrong, but it does mean you need considerably more evidence before committing serious time to it.

## Using the classification

The point of classifying isn't to file your idea into a category and move on — it's to calibrate how much scepticism to apply to yourself before you commit. Copy ideas can move quickly with light validation. Improve ideas need you to confirm both the problem and your specific fix. Differentiate ideas deserve real, patient evidence-gathering before significant investment, because the base rate of "asking people to do something new" actually working out is lower than founders like to believe.

Once you know which category your idea falls into, the next step is scoring how strong your actual evidence is — not just how you'd classify the idea in principle. That's what the Product Idea Assessor Tool does: it takes your classification plus four evidence questions and turns them into a scored readiness verdict, a named strongest and weakest area, and one concrete next action. If you'd rather work through it on paper first, the Copy–Improve–Differentiate Assessment template covers the same ground.
