Here's an uncomfortable test: think back to your last five customer interviews. Did any of them change your mind about anything? Not confirm something you suspected — actually change it. If the honest answer is no, the interviews weren't doing their job. They were theatre.

Customer interviews have a specific failure mode that's almost invisible from the inside. You ask questions shaped by what you already believe, the other person picks up on that shape and answers agreeably, and you leave with your existing view intact, now labelled "validated." The interview felt productive. It wasn't.

Here's how to avoid that.

## Interview people about their past, not your future

The single biggest lever you have is what you ask about. Questions about the future — "would you use this," "how much would you pay," "do you think this is a good idea" — invite speculation. People are bad at predicting their own future behaviour, and they're polite, so speculation tends to drift toward whatever answer seems kind.

Questions about the past are different. "Tell me about the last time you dealt with [the problem]" doesn't invite an opinion — it asks for a memory. Memories are much harder to fake or soften, and they come with detail: what actually happened, what they tried, how long it took, whether it worked. That detail is where the real evidence lives.

Build your interview guide almost entirely around the past and present: what do they do today, what have they tried, what's the actual cost — in time, money or frustration — of the current situation. Save any forward-looking questions for the very end, and treat the answers as colour, not evidence.

## Stop pitching before the interview even starts

If you open with a description of your idea, you've already contaminated the conversation — the interviewee now knows what answer would please you, consciously or not. Get as far as possible into understanding their situation before you say anything about what you're building. In many good interviews, you never mention it at all.

This feels wasteful in the moment. It isn't. An interview where you learn what someone's actual workflow looks like, unprompted by your idea, is worth more than five interviews where you describe your product and collect nods.

## Ask "why" more than feels natural

Most first answers are surface-level. "We use a spreadsheet for that" is a fact, not an insight. The insight is usually one or two "why" questions deeper: why a spreadsheet and not the tool that's supposedly built for this? What did they try before the spreadsheet? What would have to be true for them to switch off it?

You don't need to interrogate anyone — a simple, curious "what made you choose that?" or "what's annoying about that?" usually does it. The goal is to get past the first, rehearsed answer to the specific detail underneath it.

## Write down what surprised you, immediately

Directly after each interview — not at the end of the day, not after you've done three more — write one sentence: what did I hear that I didn't expect? If you can't answer that, be honest with yourself about whether the interview actually taught you anything, or just kept you busy.

Keep these surprises somewhere alongside your running list of assumptions and evidence. Over five or ten interviews, a pattern in your "surprised by" notes is usually more valuable than the transcripts themselves — it's pointing directly at where your mental model of the customer was wrong.

## Know when you've heard enough

You don't need a statistically significant sample to make a good early decision. You need to stop hearing new things. When three or four interviews in a row confirm the same pattern with no new surprises, you probably have enough to act on. When every interview still surfaces something you didn't expect, you're not done — keep going, because you're still learning.
