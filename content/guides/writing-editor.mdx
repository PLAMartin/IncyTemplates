Most founders write everything themselves — blog posts, emails, positioning statements, the pitch. The problem isn't the first draft. It's that the person who wrote it is the worst-placed person to spot what's wrong with it. You can't unsee your own intentions; you read what you meant to say, not what's actually on the page. Editing well means two separate things: creating enough distance to read your own words like a stranger would, then knowing what to look for once you can.

## Fresh eyes first

Austin Kleon, reflecting on his own drafting process, uses three techniques before he shares anything. **Give it time** — set the draft aside for at least a day and come back with fresh eyes; ideas sharpen when you stop staring at them. **Print it out** — a hard copy, marked up in pencil, feels like someone else wrote it, which is exactly the distance you need to critique it honestly. **Read it aloud** — your ear catches repetitions, clumsy phrases and dead rhythm that your eye slides straight past on a silent page.

None of these change a single word. They just put you in a position to actually see the words that are there.

## Then five rules for the prose itself

Once you can read it fresh, George Orwell's rules — laid out in *Politics and the English Language* — give you something concrete to check for. He believed clarity wasn't a stylistic preference but a moral one: vague language doesn't just confuse, it conceals. Five of his six rules are directly checkable in a draft:

**Never use a metaphor, simile or figure of speech you're used to seeing in print.** Overused phrases like "tip of the iceberg" have lost their impact through repetition — say the thing directly instead: "a small part of a much larger problem."

**Never use a long word where a short one will do.** Clear communication beats inflated vocabulary. Don't say "utilise" when "use" will do; say "help" instead of "facilitate," "buy" instead of "procure."

**If it's possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.** Most prose is bloated. "Due to the fact that" becomes "Because." Tight writing respects the reader's time.

**Never use the passive where you can use the active.** "The meeting was led by Jane" becomes "Jane led the meeting" — the active voice is more dynamic and puts the reader in no doubt about who did what.

**Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday equivalent.** "In vitro solution leveraging scalable architecture" becomes "A lab-made fix that works at scale." Complex language obscures meaning to sound impressive; good writing is accessible.

Orwell's sixth rule is really about the other five: **break any of them sooner than say anything outright barbarous.** They exist to serve clarity, not to become dogma — if following one makes your meaning less clear, break it.

## Using the process

The **Self-Edit Checklist** is a one-page, printable version of both halves — Kleon's three steps and Orwell's five rules — to run through before you publish or send anything. The **Structured Editing Review** Tool asks five quick yes/no questions about a specific draft, one per Orwell rule, and gives you a fix tip and before/after example for whichever rule is still flagged first.

This isn't a one-off pass. Run it on the next thing you write, and the one after that — the rules stay the same, but you'll get faster at spotting the problems yourself each time.
