Most products fail not because they were built badly, but because they were built for a market that never really wanted them. Surveys and opinions can't tell you that — people are generous with their opinions and stingy with their money. Pretotyping answers a narrower, more honest question: if this thing existed, would anyone actually care? You measure that through behaviour — clicks, signups, real engagement — not through what people say they'd do.

## Four ways to test before you build

**Fake Door Test**: a landing page with a Buy Now or Sign Up button, before the product exists. When someone clicks, you've measured real intent, not a hypothetical opinion. **Wizard of Oz**: offer a working service, but do the work manually behind the scenes — the way DoorDash's founders first fulfilled delivery orders themselves before building any technology. **YouTube MVP**: film a short video showing how your idea would work, the way Dropbox's founder demonstrated file-syncing in a video years before the product was ready, and track views, shares and signups. **The Infiltrator**: place your idea inside an existing ecosystem — Amazon, Etsy, Airbnb, wherever your customers already look — and watch whether people actually engage with it.

## Set the bar before you test

Define what would actually count as a signal before you run the test, using a Market Engagement Hypothesis: *if we offer [idea] to [target market], at least [X%] will [take action] within [Y time]*. Decide the threshold in advance — it stops you from moving the goalposts after you see the result, in either direction. If the test clears the bar, you've got early evidence of demand. If it doesn't, you haven't failed — you've learned something real, cheaply, before spending months building it.

## Worked example: Shift Swap

Priya's discovery interviews surfaced a real day-of pain point, but also a warning sign: a previous shared-spreadsheet attempt had died from nobody trusting or checking it. Before building anything, she wants to know whether people will actually act on a new shared board, not just say they'd try it.

Her Market Engagement Hypothesis: *if we offer Shift Swap to independent café and retail managers in our city, at least 15% of the people we reach will sign up for early access within one week.* She sets that threshold before running anything, so she can't quietly lower the bar if the result disappoints her.

She chooses a **Fake Door Test**: a one-page description of Shift Swap — "post a shift, get it covered in minutes" — with a "Get early access" button, shared in three local small-business groups and directly with everyone she'd interviewed. She considered a Wizard of Oz version (matching swap requests manually behind a form) but rejected it — manually matching would only prove demand at one location, not tell her whether the idea travels beyond the specific people she already knows. The Fake Door Test clears her 15% bar comfortably: 22 signups from roughly 90 people reached, most of them managers rather than workers — itself a useful signal about who to design the first version for.
